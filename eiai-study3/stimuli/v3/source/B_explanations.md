## MODULE 1 — Your body in space

[1] CLAIM: In weightlessness, astronauts grow taller — by up to about 3% of their height.

STRONG — evidence type: mechanism + direct measurement + reversal
In weightlessness, astronauts grow taller — by up to about 3% of their height. On Earth, gravity squeezes the soft discs between the vertebrae; in orbit, the discs expand and the spine lengthens. After 340 days on the ISS, NASA astronaut Scott Kelly came back about 4 cm taller than before launch. Within two days on Earth, gravity had pressed him back to his usual height.

WEAK — core: A3
In weightlessness, astronauts grow taller — by up to about 3% of their height. Space physiologists call this spinal elongation, and it is one of the effects studied by NASA's Human Research Program. [A3, B1] The ISS has been continuously occupied since November 2000, giving researchers more than two decades of crew health records. [A2] Before launch, every Soyuz crew member also gets a seat liner custom-molded to their body. [A1]
FLAW: Names the effect and mentions research, but never says what was measured.

[2] CLAIM: In space, food tastes blander than on Earth.

STRONG — evidence type: mechanism chain + behavioral corroboration
In space, food tastes blander than on Earth. Without gravity, about two liters of body fluid shift toward the head, and the congestion feels like a constant head cold. Much of what we experience as flavor comes from smell, so a blocked nose dulls it. This matches what NASA reports about crew behavior: astronauts regularly ask for hot sauces and other strong condiments.

WEAK — core: C1 + C2
In space, food tastes blander than on Earth. A study by Germany's Fraunhofer Institute for Lufthansa found that in airplane cabin conditions, salt tastes up to 30% weaker. [C1, B1, A2] That's why many airlines now season in-flight meals more strongly to make up for the loss. [C1] The ISS flies about 400 km up, more than 30 times higher than a cruising airliner. [C2]
FLAW: The airplane effect comes from low cabin pressure and dry air; the ISS is kept at sea-level pressure, so altitude doesn't carry over.

[3] CLAIM: In weightlessness, astronauts' faces get puffy and their legs get thinner.

STRONG — evidence type: mechanism + measurement + reversal
In weightlessness, astronauts' faces get puffy and their legs get thinner. On Earth, gravity pulls blood and other fluids toward the feet, and the body constantly works against that pull. In orbit, the pull disappears but the body keeps pushing, so about two liters of fluid move from the legs toward the head within a day or two. NASA notes that within about three days after landing, the fluid returns to where it was.

WEAK — core: E1 + E2
In weightlessness, astronauts' faces get puffy and their legs get thinner. For years, the standard ISS menu contained more than 5,000 mg of sodium a day, until NASA reformulated about 90 foods to cut it. [E1, A2, B1] Crew members also sleep in cabins the size of a phone booth, often for only about six hours a night. [E1] And the station's air is constantly dehumidified so the moisture can be recycled into drinking water. [E1, E2]
FLAW: Three true facts presented as causes; none explains the fluid shift or the thinner legs, and dry air contradicts swelling.

[4] CLAIM: In weightlessness, astronauts lose about 1–1.5% of the bone mass in their hips and spine every month.

STRONG — evidence type: direct measurement + mechanism + intervention
In weightlessness, astronauts lose about 1–1.5% of the bone mass in their hips and spine every month. Bone constantly rebuilds itself to match the load it carries, and in orbit the legs and spine carry almost none. NASA scans taken before and after long ISS missions show the loss directly. Two hours of daily exercise on the station's resistance machine slows it down but does not fully stop it.

WEAK — core: D2 + C2
In weightlessness, astronauts lose about 1–1.5% of the bone mass in their hips and spine every month. On Earth, older adults gradually lose around 1% of their bone mass per year. [D2, A2] Spaceflight is often described as a model of accelerated aging, since many changes in orbit resemble those seen in the elderly. [C2, C4] NASA's bone research is even used to help doctors understand osteoporosis. [B1]
FLAW: Borrows a yearly rate from older adults plus a metaphor; nothing here measures astronauts' bones.

## MODULE 2 — Life aboard the orbital station

[5] CLAIM: In weightlessness, a candle flame becomes round and blue instead of tall and yellow.

STRONG — evidence type: mechanism + direct observation
In weightlessness, a candle flame becomes round and blue instead of tall and yellow. On Earth, rising hot air stretches the flame upward and carries glowing soot to its tip; in orbit, hot air doesn't rise, so the flame forms evenly around the wick. NASA tested this on the Space Shuttle in 1992 and on the Mir station in 1995. The flames were small, about 1.5 cm across, and dim blue.

WEAK — core: C3
In weightlessness, a candle flame becomes round and blue instead of tall and yellow. Researchers call this field microgravity combustion, and NASA has run flame experiments in orbit for decades. [A3, B1] Fire is one of the most serious emergencies on any spacecraft: in 1997, a fire broke out aboard the Russian station Mir. [C3, D1] Today, the ISS carries portable fire extinguishers and smoke detectors throughout its modules, and crews practice emergency drills. [C3]
FLAW: Switches to fire safety and names the research field, but never explains or shows the flame's shape.

[6] CLAIM: In weightlessness, tears don't roll down your face — they collect into a ball of water on your eye.

STRONG — evidence type: mechanism + demonstration
In weightlessness, tears don't roll down your face — they collect into a ball of water on your eye. On Earth, gravity pulls a drop off the eye once it gets heavy enough; in orbit, only surface tension acts, and it holds the water in place. In 2013, Canadian astronaut Chris Hadfield demonstrated this on the ISS. The water he put on his eye formed a growing ball that stayed there until he wiped it away.

WEAK — core: C4
In weightlessness, tears don't roll down your face — they collect into a ball of water on your eye. In orbit, body fluids shift toward the head, which is why astronauts' faces look puffy. [C4] With no gravity pulling downward, fluids around the eyes tend to stay where they are. [C4] Eye changes are so common on six-month missions that NASA gave them their own name: spaceflight-associated neuro-ocular syndrome, or SANS. [A3, A1, B1]
FLAW: Mixes up fluid inside the body with water on the eye's surface; the syndrome's name has nothing to do with tears.

[7] CLAIM: Astronauts on the ISS see about 16 sunrises every day.

STRONG — evidence type: calculation
Astronauts on the ISS see about 16 sunrises every day. According to NASA, the station circles Earth roughly every 90 minutes, and 24 hours divided by 90 minutes gives 16 orbits. On every orbit, the ISS passes through Earth's shadow once and then comes back out into sunlight. Each time it leaves the shadow, the crew sees the Sun rise over the horizon.

WEAK — core: B2 + E1
Astronauts on the ISS see about 16 sunrises every day. The station travels at roughly 28,000 km/h, fast enough to cross an ocean in minutes. [A2, A1] To keep sleep on track, crews live on Greenwich Mean Time, and NASA installed LED lights that shift color over the course of a "day." [B2, E1] Many astronauts also wear sleep masks, since sunlight can reach the windows at unexpected hours. [B2, E1]
FLAW: Describes how crews cope with light, which assumes the claim instead of showing it; speed alone doesn't give the length of an orbit.

[8] CLAIM: About 98% of the water on the ISS is recovered and reused — including water from urine and sweat.

STRONG — evidence type: direct report + mechanism
About 98% of the water on the ISS is recovered and reused — including water from urine and sweat. The station's life-support system collects moisture from the cabin air, distills urine, and filters the result into drinking water. In 2023, NASA reported that a new brine processor raised total recovery from 93–94% to 98%. It works by extracting the last water from the concentrated leftovers of urine processing.

WEAK — core: C7
About 98% of the water on the ISS is recovered and reused — including water from urine and sweat. Cargo ships reach the station only several times a year, yet no crew has ever had to leave the ISS because it ran out of water. [C7, A2] Each crew member needs roughly 4 liters of water a day for drinking, food, and hygiene. [A2] The station's recycling hardware was developed largely at NASA's Marshall Space Flight Center in Alabama. [B1]
FLAW: Not running out shows there was enough water, not that 98% is recycled — deliveries could cover the gap.

## MODULE 3 — On Mars

[9] CLAIM: Sunsets on Mars look blue.

STRONG — evidence type: direct images + mechanism
Sunsets on Mars look blue. NASA's Curiosity rover photographed this from Gale Crater in 2015, and earlier rovers captured it too. The fine Martian dust scatters blue light forward, close to the direction the sunlight is traveling. So when you look toward the setting Sun, the sky right around it glows blue, while the rest of the sky stays reddish.

WEAK — core: C6
Sunsets on Mars look blue. On Earth, it works the other way around: the daytime sky is blue, and sunsets turn orange and red. [C6] Mars is known as the Red Planet, and its daytime sky has a pale butterscotch color, so the pattern is essentially reversed. [C6, A1] NASA's Curiosity rover has been photographing the Martian sky from Gale Crater since 2012. [B1]
FLAW: Infers the sunset color from a "reversed pattern," which is not evidence; the photos are mentioned but not what they showed.

[10] CLAIM: Dust storms on Mars can't knock over equipment; the real danger is dust covering solar panels.

STRONG — evidence type: mechanism + event with causal chain
Dust storms on Mars can't knock over equipment; the real danger is dust covering solar panels. Martian air is only about 1% as dense as Earth's, so even a 100 km/h storm pushes about as hard as a 10 km/h breeze on Earth. Dust, however, piles up: in 2018, a planet-wide storm covered the solar panels of NASA's Opportunity rover. Without enough sunlight to recharge, the rover lost power and never recovered.

WEAK — core: C8 + D1 + E2
Dust storms on Mars can't knock over equipment; the real danger is dust covering solar panels. NASA's Opportunity rover was designed to last about 90 days, yet it kept working for more than 14 years. [A1, A2] In 2018 it went silent during a planet-wide dust storm, and NASA ended the mission the following year after many attempts to reach it. [D1, C8] For years before that, gusts of wind had regularly blown dust off its panels. [E2]
FLAW: Never addresses wind force; "went silent during a storm" doesn't show why, and the wind-cleaning fact pulls the other way.

[11] CLAIM: Mars has sound, but it is much quieter and travels slower than on Earth — about 240 m/s instead of 343.

STRONG — evidence type: direct measurement + observation
Mars has sound, but it is much quieter and travels slower than on Earth — about 240 m/s instead of 343. NASA's Perseverance rover carries microphones, and in 2022 scientists published measurements of how fast the sound of its own laser traveled to them. Mars turned out to be so quiet that the team several times thought a microphone had stopped working. The thin, cold, carbon dioxide air carries sound weakly and slowly.

WEAK — core: C5
Mars has sound, but it is much quieter and travels slower than on Earth — about 240 m/s instead of 343. In the vacuum of space there is no sound at all, because there is nothing for it to travel through. [A1] Mars sits in between: according to NASA, its surface air pressure is less than 1% of Earth's, around 600 pascals. [C5, A2, B1] So its sound properties land somewhere between Earth's and empty space. [C5]
FLAW: Treats Mars as a "midpoint" between Earth and vacuum; the speed of sound depends on the gas and its temperature, not on being "in between."

[12] CLAIM: A radio message between Earth and Mars takes about 3 to 22 minutes to arrive, depending on where the planets are.

STRONG — evidence type: calculation + consequence
A radio message between Earth and Mars takes about 3 to 22 minutes to arrive, depending on where the planets are. Radio waves travel at the speed of light, about 300,000 km per second. At their closest, the planets are about 55 million km apart, and at their farthest about 400 million km, which gives roughly 3 and 22 minutes. During Curiosity's landing in 2012, the delay was about 14 minutes, so the rover had to land on its own.

WEAK — core: D1 + C4
A radio message between Earth and Mars takes about 3 to 22 minutes to arrive, depending on where the planets are. NASA engineers called Curiosity's 2012 landing "seven minutes of terror," because all they could do was watch and wait. [C4, D1] Messages to Mars go out through NASA's Deep Space Network, with giant antennas in California, Spain, and Australia. [A1, B1] The two planets come closest to each other about every 26 months. [A2]
FLAW: "Seven minutes of terror" was the length of the landing itself, not the signal delay (about 14 minutes then); the other facts give no distances.
